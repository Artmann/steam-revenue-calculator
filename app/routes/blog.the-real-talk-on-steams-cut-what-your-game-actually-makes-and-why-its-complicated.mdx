# The Real Talk on Steam's Cut: What Your Game Actually Makes (And Why It's Complicated)

Every indie dev has heard it: "Steam takes 30%." Sell your game for $20, Steam keeps $6, you keep $14. It sounds simple, and if that were the whole story this would be a very short post. But the price on your store page and the money that lands in your bank account are two different numbers, and the gap between them is bigger than most people expect.

Start with the part that works in your favor. Steam's cut isn't fixed at 30%. Once a game passes $10 million in sales, Steam's share drops to 25% for everything between $10 million and $50 million, and to 20% after that. Most of us will never get near those numbers, so for the rest of this post, assume 30%. It's still worth knowing the tiers exist, because they show that the cut is a business decision, not a law of nature.

The first thing that eats into that $20 is regional pricing. Your game doesn't cost $20 everywhere. Steam suggests lower prices for many countries, and players there expect them, so the same game might sell for around $4.50 in Argentina or $6 in Turkey. Steam still takes its 30% of whatever the player actually paid. A friend let me share the numbers from their $15 game: about 40% of their sales came from the US and EU at full price, 35% from mid-tier regions at roughly 60% of full price, and 25% from lower-tier regions at about 30%. That works out to an average of about $10.30 per sale, not $15, and about $7.20 after Steam's cut.

Then there's tax. In many regions, VAT or sales tax comes out before Steam takes its share. Most EU countries charge 20–25% VAT, so a €20 sale can lose up to €4 to tax first. Steam then takes 30% of what's left, which leaves you with about €11. In the US it depends on the state: some don't tax digital goods at all, others charge 4–10%. Steam collects and pays all of this for you, which is a real help, but you still need to account for it when you estimate what you'll earn.

Sales are the next factor, and they cut both ways. A discount comes out of your share, not Steam's, because Steam takes its 30% of the discounted price. Put your $20 game on sale at 33% off and it sells for $13.40. Steam keeps $4.02 and you get $9.38. Add regional pricing and tax on top, and in some regions you might see $5–6 per copy during a sale. The upside is volume. A well-timed sale can multiply your unit sales by five or more, and many indie devs make 40–50% of their yearly revenue during the big Steam sale events. Whether a sale is worth it comes down to whether the extra copies make up for the lower price per copy.

Here's how that plays out for a real game. "Dungeon Cats" (not its real name) launched in 2023 at $19.99 and sold 50,000 copies in its first month. That's about $1,000,000 in gross revenue. Regional pricing brought it down to around $800,000, and after Steam's cut the studio kept about $560,000. That's a great result, but it came after three years of work by a team of four, plus $50,000 in marketing and $20,000 in engine licenses and tools. Successful, yes, but the headline number isn't what the team took home.

So what should you plan for? A reasonable rule of thumb is that you'll keep 50–60% of your list price per copy once everything is taken out, and 30–40% during heavy sales. If the sales bring in enough extra players, that lower share can still be the right call. And for all the talk about the 30%, Steam does give you something for it: a huge player base, community features, automatic updates, payment processing in dozens of regions, and fraud protection. The cut isn't going anywhere, but knowing how it stacks up with regional pricing, tax and discounts lets you plan with real numbers instead of the price on your store page.
